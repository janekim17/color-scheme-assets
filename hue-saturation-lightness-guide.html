Hue, saturation and lightness explained

“Make it lighter” and “make it less vivid” describe different changes. Here is how to tell them apart.

By Color Scheme Studio · October 21, 2026

Three questions about one colour

What colour family is it? How vivid is it? How light or dark is it? Hue, saturation and lightness give you separate ways to describe those differences.

HueSaturationLightnessTry itWhat the numbers miss

Hue: which colour family?

Hue distinguishes a blue from a green or an orange. In the HSL model used here, it is an angle around a circle: 0° is red, 120° is green and 240° is blue. At 360° you arrive back at red. The angle is a position, not a score for how strong or bright the colour is.

Look at the three samples below. Their saturation and HSL lightness stay the same; only the hue changes. This is useful when you mean “more green” rather than “lighter” or “less intense.”

Saturation: vivid or muted?

In HSL, reducing saturation moves a colour toward gray while keeping its lightness setting fixed. At 0% saturation it is gray, so changing hue has no visible effect. At 100%, saturation is at its maximum for that hue and HSL lightness.

Compare the samples from left to right. The blue becomes more pronounced, but this is not a simple journey from dark to light. If a colour feels too loud, reducing saturation is a different adjustment from adding a pale tint.

Lightness: toward black or white?

HSL lightness runs from black at 0% to white at 100%. Between those extremes, the same hue can appear deep, clear or pale. At either endpoint, hue and saturation no longer make a visible difference.

Watch the middle samples as well as the endpoints. Raising lightness can make a blue look pastel even when its saturation number stays high. That is why “pale” and “low saturation” are not interchangeable descriptions.

Change one thing at a time

Start with the blue below. Move saturation toward zero, then change hue. The gray should remain gray. Reset, then move lightness toward white: this time the colour becomes pale instead. The number beside each slider describes exactly what you are changing.

The same lightness number can look different

HSL is convenient for describing changes, but its lightness scale is not perceptually uniform. A yellow and a blue with the same HSL lightness do not necessarily look equally light. These two samples both use 100% saturation and 50% lightness:

Do not treat the lightness slider as a text-readability meter. Check the actual foreground and background if you need readable text. Also notice how the surrounding surface affects your impression; a colour value describes the sample, not every condition in which someone will see it.

Use more precise colour language

Before moving a slider, name the difference you want. “More violet” asks for a hue shift. “Less vivid” asks for reduced saturation. “Closer to white” asks for increased lightness. Make that single change first, compare it with the original, and then decide whether another adjustment is needed. You do not need to solve a full colour scheme to learn what is happening to one colour.
