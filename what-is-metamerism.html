What is metamerism? When matching colors stop matching

Two materials can look like a perfect colour match in one room and disagree in another. Sometimes the match depends on the light.

By Color Scheme Studio · October 27, 2026

A match under one light is a conditional match.

Metamerism describes a pair of colours that match in one viewing condition but no longer match in another. This guide focuses on physical samples when the illumination changes.

What it meansWhy light mattersCompare samplesWhat a screen cannot tell you

Start with two samples, not one changing colour

Imagine choosing a fabric beside a painted colour sample. They look alike under the light where you make the selection. Later, under a different lamp, one seems greener while the other seems warmer. The important observation is that their relationship has changed: the pair matched before, and now it does not.

That is the situation called illuminant metamerism. A single surface looking warmer in warm light is not enough to demonstrate it. You need a comparison between samples and a match that breaks when the viewing condition changes. The example here keeps the observer the same and changes the light; other forms of metamerism exist, but they are outside this introduction.

The materials can reflect light differently

A physical colour reaches you through a combination of illumination, the material and your visual response. Two materials can reflect different proportions of the wavelengths falling on them yet look alike in a particular condition. A changed light source can expose the difference between those reflection patterns.

This explains why copying a colour name or approving one quick visual match may be insufficient when different materials must agree. A printed label, a fabric ribbon and a coated box are made differently. A close match in one meeting room does not establish that their match will survive the lighting where the finished set is displayed.

The light itself needs more description than “warm” or “cool.” Two lamps with the same colour temperature can have different spectral distributions and render materials differently. Choosing a lamp only by its Kelvin label does not establish a controlled colour-evaluation setup. For critical work, agree on the relevant viewing conditions with the supplier rather than substituting whichever desk lamp is available.

Make a small, repeatable comparison

For an ordinary design decision, start with the actual pieces you need to match. Keep them together, with comparable orientation and a simple surrounding surface. Look under one light at a time. Mixed window light and room lighting can make it difficult to describe the condition in which you saw a difference.

  1. Identify the pair. Note which two physical samples you are comparing. A phone picture of one piece is not a replacement for the piece itself.
  2. Record the first condition. Say where you compared them and what light was used. Record “match” or describe the visible difference in plain words.
  3. Repeat under relevant lighting. Compare them together in the room, shop or display setting where they will be used. Keep other conditions as consistent as practical.
  4. Agree before approving. If the pair separates, show the supplier both samples and describe the conditions. Keep an agreed reference instead of approving from memory.

This is a useful screening exercise, not a laboratory measurement. A discrepancy can also involve texture, gloss, viewing angle or other factors. Do not diagnose every mismatch as metamerism simply because you moved to another room. Where matching is commercially important, controlled visual evaluation and appropriate spectral measurement provide a stronger basis for the decision.

A HEX code cannot certify a physical match

The diagram above makes the idea visible, but a web page cannot replace two real material samples under real lights. Its rectangles are light emitted by your display. They have no fabric, ink or paint reflection data attached to them. Copying one rectangle’s HEX value into a production brief would specify a screen colour, not prove that two manufactured surfaces will match.

Likewise, a photograph adds the camera, exposure, white balance, processing and display to the comparison. A photo can document a concern, but a difference in two photos does not by itself establish what happened to the material match. When you need a production decision, return to the samples and the agreed viewing setup.

Ask a more useful approval question

Instead of asking only “Is this the right colour?”, ask “Does this pair match under the conditions that matter?” That question is useful for a ribbon beside a box, a print beside a painted panel, or replacement fabric beside an existing piece. It keeps the decision tied to the actual objects and their destination, rather than treating a colour label as a promise that every material will behave identically.
