Color 101

Start with a role for each color

A palette becomes useful when each color has a job. Choose a broad background, a readable text color, a supporting surface, and an accent. You do not need to use every swatch in equal amounts, or even use all five in the same design.

Hue, saturation, and lightness

Hue describes the color family, such as green or blue. Saturation describes how vivid or muted a color appears. Lightness helps separate elements: two different hues with similar lightness may still be difficult to distinguish in small text or a chart.

Hex, RGB, and CMYK

Hex is a compact way to write RGB values for a screen. RGB mixes red, green, and blue light. CMYK describes cyan, magenta, yellow, and black ink. The CMYK numbers shown on this site are approximate conversions; a printer’s color profile and paper choice affect the final result.

Check contrast in context

An attractive photograph does not automatically produce accessible text colors. Test foreground and background pairs at the font size and weight you will use. For normal text, WCAG AA uses a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1; large text uses 3:1. Do not communicate an important state with color alone.

Try a small layout first

Make one card, one invitation, or one sample page before extending a palette across a project. A small test reveals whether the accent is too strong, the neutrals are too similar, or the background competes with the image.
