Tints, shades and tones: what is the difference?

Three ways to change a colour, and why “lighter” and “less vivid” are not the same request.

By Color Scheme Studio · October 25, 2026

Start with what is being added.

In conventional mixing language, white makes a tint, black makes a shade, and gray makes a tone. These are descriptions of changes, not three unrelated colour families.

Three termsCompare the changesWhat the numbers meanTry it yourself

One starting colour, three directions

A tint moves a starting colour toward white. A shade moves it toward black. A tone mixes it with gray, which contains both white and black. This is a common way painters and designers distinguish the three words. In everyday speech, “shade” may simply mean any version of a colour, so it is useful to state which meaning you intend.

Imagine a blue that feels too forceful. A pale blue tint and a muted blue tone are two different answers. The tint becomes lighter as it approaches white. A tone becomes more neutral as it approaches the chosen gray. A shade heads in a darker direction. None of those changes is automatically the right one; the term helps you describe the change before you judge its appearance.

Compare the three rows

Each row starts with the same blue, #3478A7. The target is different: white, black or middle gray. Read each row from left to right and compare the halfway samples, rather than looking only at the endpoints.

The middle tint is a pale blue, while the middle shade is deep and dark. The middle tone is closer to a blue-gray. The rows make one point clear: moving toward white and moving toward gray can both soften a colour, but they do not produce the same result.

These percentages describe this diagram

“50% added” here means a halfway average of the starting and target sRGB channel numbers. For example, halfway between blue #3478A7 and white gives #9ABCD3. The labels describe that calculation only. They do not promise an equally spaced visual scale, a particular HSL saturation value or a recipe for mixing physical paint.

Real pigments have their own behaviour, and different digital blending spaces can produce different intermediate colours. Treat these rows as a controlled screen demonstration. If you mix paint, compare real swatches and record the pigments used. If you work digitally, keep the blending method consistent while comparing alternatives.

“Tint” can also have a production-specific meaning. In print software, a tint of a spot colour usually refers to using a percentage of that ink, rather than physically mixing white ink into it. Context matters: a painting description, a screen colour and a print swatch do not use identical production methods.

Make three alternatives before choosing one

Duplicate a single colour into three samples. Move one toward white, one toward black and one toward a neutral gray. Keep their size and surroundings the same, and label the adjustment. Then put the samples beside the colour you began with. Decide whether the difference you wanted was lightness, darkness or a more muted appearance.

Finally, test the selected version in the place where it will appear. A very pale tint can be pleasant as a large field yet disappear as a thin line. A shade can work for a strong block and feel too heavy across a whole page. The words help you make a precise change; the final size, neighbouring colours and reading conditions still need to be judged in context.
