Color 101 / One palette, several formats

How to create a wedding color palette across digital and print

Choose a few stable color roles, then let a website, a small card, and a paper keepsake use different amounts of them.

By Color Scheme Studio · October 19, 2026

In this guideStart with the briefCompare formatsChoose reading inkPlan the print handoff
The useful starting point

Keep the color identity consistent, not the percentage of every swatch. One blue can connect the set while the reading surface and amount of decoration change with the format.

Begin with the occasion and the information

A wedding palette has to support more than a mood board. Guests need names, dates, locations, and practical instructions. A keepsake can be quieter and more decorative. Before choosing which shade fills the largest area, list the pieces you actually need and how someone will use each one.

The hand-in-hand portrait behind our spring palette offers a useful relationship: a small cool blue area beside warm fabric and pale stone. A wedding application can borrow that balance without claiming the photograph records a wedding. Treat it as visual inspiration, then use your own occasion’s wording and materials.

Choose an identity cue and two useful neutrals

Vow Blue can be the element people recognize across the set. Linen White gives digital text space; Sandstone Veil creates a warmer inset or paper-facing panel. Terracotta Thread and Apricot Silk can stay in small motifs instead of becoming competing headline colors.

Make a compact role record: identity, primary reading surface, secondary surface, warm detail, and supporting text ink. Record HEX values for the screen version. A custom color name such as “Vow Blue” is convenient shorthand for the project, not a standardized ink or fabric reference.

Keep the details together

Website: linen is the reading surface. A blue action provides the repeated identity cue.

A website can give each practical section its own breathing room. A small card has fewer lines to work with, so a shorter title and clear spacing matter. A sleeve can carry a broader sandstone field because its main task is to identify the set. Equal swatches do not imply equal area on every piece.

Add a reading ink instead of forcing the five colors

This soft palette has no deep text neutral. Supporting ink #283D42 gives the words a separate role. On Vow Blue it reaches 5.55:1; white on that same blue reaches 2.06:1. A white headline may look light and airy but does not meet the normal-text contrast threshold in that pairing.

Use the same dark ink for names and practical details across the set, then vary size, spacing, and position to establish hierarchy. Keep the exact text pair in your record. Dark ink on clay is a different test, so do not move the checked blue-button treatment onto the terracotta shade without checking it again.

Decorative script is especially sensitive to size and stroke weight. Try the real names, a longer address, and the smallest intended caption. Passing a color calculation does not guarantee that a delicate typeface remains easy to read after printing or reducing the design.

Prepare a print reference rather than a CMYK promise

The photograph’s pale stone is not a measurement of paper white. A cream stock changes the apparent warmth of an unprinted area, and printing pale blue on it can look different from the screen mockup. Choose the stock with the printer before treating the digital preview as a final material reference.

Ask for the required output profile and export settings. Keep the editable RGB master and prepare the print file to that specification. Some workflows handle RGB conversion at the print stage; others ask for a specific CMYK profile. Do not substitute a generic four-number conversion for the printer’s instructions.

Compare a physical proof when the color matters. Look at the pale blue, small dark text, warm motif, and unprinted stock together. If the warm colors merge or the text loses detail, adjust the print version deliberately while keeping a record of what changed. A screen and a sheet of paper do not produce light in the same way.

Keep a small handoff sheet

  • The five inspiration swatches and their roles.
  • The supporting ink and checked digital text/background pairs.
  • One website example and one small-card example with real-length copy.
  • The chosen stock, printer’s output instructions, and proof status.
  • The photograph’s creator and original source link.

For a related exercise, read how to balance a five-color palette. The goal is a recognizable family of useful pieces, not a rule that every object must look identical.
