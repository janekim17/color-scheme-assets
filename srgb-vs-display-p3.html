sRGB vs Display P3: what changes on your screen?

A wider colour space can describe more vivid colours. It does not make every image more accurate or every screen look the same.

By Color Scheme Studio · October 25, 2026

Colour numbers need a colour space.

sRGB and Display P3 give RGB values a defined meaning. Display P3 includes colours outside sRGB, but the file, software and display all matter.

A gamut is a range, not a quality score

A colour gamut describes a range of colours. The Display P3 gamut includes the sRGB gamut and extends beyond it, particularly into some more saturated colours. That larger range is useful when the source really contains such colours and the output can show them. It does not mean every colour in a P3 document should be more saturated.

A quiet gray or a muted blue can be represented in either space. If a colour is already within the shared range, simply choosing a wider space does not add useful colour information to it. Think about the colours your work needs, rather than choosing a space because its name sounds more advanced.

The same RGB triplet can mean a different colour

Consider the label “red: 1, green: 0, blue: 0.” Its meaning depends on the colour space. The red primary of Display P3 differs from the red primary of sRGB. Copying three channel numbers between the spaces without a conversion can therefore change the intended colour.

A colour-managed conversion tries to preserve appearance as it translates between spaces. Assigning a different profile instead changes how existing numbers are interpreted. Those are different operations. When an image suddenly looks stronger after a profile change, investigate whether its values were converted or merely reinterpreted; do not assume the extra saturation is a faithful improvement.

Two specified reds, with an honest limitation

The left sample uses sRGB red. The right requests the Display P3 red primary when the browser understands that notation, with sRGB as a fallback. A wide-gamut display may show a difference. On another screen they may look alike. Neither outcome is a display calibration test.

A screenshot of this example may be converted before it reaches someone else. Judge the live example on the device in question, and keep the limitation in mind. Browser support for a colour notation and the display’s ability to reproduce that colour are separate parts of the chain.

Check the destination, not just the working file

Before sending an image, identify the destination and its requirements. For a workflow that expects sRGB, export a converted sRGB copy and preserve appropriate colour information. Keep the wider-gamut original if you need it for other outputs. For a managed P3 workflow, check that the application, exported format and destination preserve the intended space.

Reopen the exported file and compare it with the original in colour-managed software. Look especially at the strongest reds, oranges and greens. If some colours are outside the destination’s gamut, the conversion has to bring them into range. Review those changes instead of repeatedly increasing saturation to chase a display you cannot control.

For a website, a usable sRGB version is a sensible foundation, with wider-gamut colour as a deliberate enhancement where supported. For print, ask for the actual output profile or production specification; neither sRGB nor Display P3 is itself a specification for a particular press and paper.
