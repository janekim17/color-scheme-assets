A winter palette with something warm inside

Snow does not make this photograph colorless. The pale snow sits against dense green leaves, while red-orange berries and amber details interrupt the cool, muted scene. Those small warm marks are what keep the composition alive. This five-color palette follows the same idea: make room for a quiet surface, anchor it with green, and let the berries supply the moment of attention.

Read the five colors as a set

Snow Veil (#D1D2D4) is a cool gray rather than a bright paper white. It gives a digital layout a softer starting surface. Winter Sage (#737E67) sits between the gray and the darker leaves, so it works as a supporting panel or illustration color. Deep Evergreen (#33462F) provides structure and readable dark text on the pale gray. Frosted Berry (#BB5041) is the strongest warm accent. Amber Twig (#AD713B) offers a quieter warm note for a line, icon, or small decorative detail. These are Color Scheme Studio’s descriptive names; the HEX values identify the screen colors.

Start with an uneven balance

Try Snow Veil across about 55% of the composition, Deep Evergreen across 25%, Winter Sage across 12%, Frosted Berry across 6%, and Amber Twig across 2%. Treat these proportions as a sketch, not a formula. A photograph already contributes color, so reduce the surrounding accents when the image becomes the focal point. Red and amber do not need equal space: one can lead while the other supplies a small echo.

From color to composition

Three places to start.

The same five colors, with different priorities. These are original layout studies, not finished products.

For a small studio or seasonal website

Use the pale gray as the page surface, deep green for headings and reading text, and sage for an illustration or supporting block. Give the red a specific job, such as one primary action. The website study uses the darkest green to make the structure visible without relying on black. Avoid giving every link a red background; repeated accents quickly lose their sense of importance.

For an invitation or winter editorial

A green-led layout gives this combination a quieter, more enclosed mood. Set the main message in pale gray, leave generous space around it, and add one small berry-colored motif. Amber can become a fine rule or a tiny printed ornament. This direction can suit a winter gathering without depending on a bright red-and-green holiday treatment. Use the actual wording and image before deciding whether the decoration needs more contrast.

For a botanical label or small package

Try a pale front label, a dark green side panel, and one red seal or marker. Sage can carry a secondary pattern. Keep the product name readable at the size someone will hold in their hand; a color that looks separated in a large mockup may disappear in small type. The label study is a design example, not a production dieline.

Check the text before you commit

Deep Evergreen on Snow Veil has a 6.75:1 contrast ratio, which meets the WCAG AA 4.5:1 threshold for normal text. White on Frosted Berry is 4.84:1 and also clears that threshold. White on Winter Sage is 4.28:1, and white on Amber Twig is 4.04:1, so those two pairs do not meet the normal-text threshold. Use the gray-and-deep-green pair for body copy and test each new background, opacity, and text treatment in context.

Take it from the screen to paper

The HEX values describe sRGB colors. The RGB and CMYK disclosure beside the image gives additional values, but the CMYK numbers are mathematical estimates without an output profile. Paper, ink, and the print process affect the result. For an invitation or package, ask the printer for the required profile and file settings, and review a proof on the intended stock.

A useful stopping point

Begin with the gray-and-green structure and introduce the warm accents only where the layout needs direction. If red and amber start competing, remove one from the first version. A successful use of this palette can be restrained: five inspiration colors do not need five equally prominent jobs.
