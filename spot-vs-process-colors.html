Spot colors vs process colors: a beginner’s print guide

One colour on your screen can be produced with one premixed ink or several process inks. The production choice matters before the file reaches the printer.

By Color Scheme Studio · October 25, 2026

Colour appearance and ink setup are different questions.

A spot colour is a separately specified premixed ink. Process printing builds colours from standard inks, commonly cyan, magenta, yellow and black.

One premixed ink, or a combination of process inks

A spot colour is a premixed ink specified separately for printing. In conventional offset work it has its own printing plate. Process colour uses standard inks in combinations, most commonly CMYK: cyan, magenta, yellow and black. Small printed dots and their coverage help create the appearance of many different colours.

A photograph contains many colour variations, so process printing is usually practical for it. A job that needs a particular named ink may use a spot colour instead. The two approaches can also coexist: a printed piece can contain CMYK photographs alongside a separately specified spot-colour element. Ask what the job needs rather than assuming one approach is always superior.

The colour you see does not reveal the ink setup

These simplified diagrams describe production structure, not a formula for matching a particular colour. The violet block is an illustrative spot ink, with no claim to represent a named ink standard. The process side shows the four ink channels conceptually; actual printed screens and coverage vary by job.

A flat-looking red rectangle in a design file could be built from process inks or assigned as a spot swatch. Its screen appearance alone cannot tell you which. Check the swatch type and the output separations. Giving a process swatch a familiar ink name does not, by itself, specify a separate premixed ink for production.

A monitor cannot approve the physical ink

The final appearance of a printed spot colour depends on the ink and the paper. A screen preview is a simulation within the display’s limits. Some spot colours also sit outside what a particular CMYK process can reproduce, so converting a spot swatch to process can change its appearance.

Use an appropriate physical reference or a printer-approved proof for colour-critical work. Confirm the stock and finish as part of the conversation: a coated reference and an uncoated result are not interchangeable promises. Avoid picking a named ink solely from an uncalibrated phone screen and expecting the printed result to be identical.

Special effects such as metallic ink make the limitation even clearer. A flat screen sample can suggest a hue, but it cannot demonstrate the way the physical surface reflects light. Discuss the effect and request a suitable sample instead of treating an RGB preview as the finished material.

Ask about the production setup before exporting

Tell the printer which elements require special colour control and ask which ink system the job will use. Confirm whether any named spot inks are expected to remain separate or be converted to process. That decision belongs in the specification, not as an accidental consequence of a last-minute export.

Inspect the output preview or separations in the final PDF workflow. An unexpected extra spot plate can be a useful clue that an old swatch survived in the file. Conversely, a job intended to preserve a spot ink should not silently lose it during conversion. Keep the colour names consistent and let the printer confirm the final setup.

Spot inks can add production steps and cost, while process colour can be more practical for complex imagery. The useful choice balances the intended appearance, available equipment, paper, quantity and budget. Agree on those details before final approval, and keep an approved reference with the job.
