Color 101 / Colour fundamentals

Analogous, complementary and triadic colors explained

Neighbours, opposites and an evenly spaced triangle: three common names describe where hues sit on a colour wheel.

By Color Scheme Studio · November 3, 2026

A relationship between hues is a starting point.

It tells you how colours are positioned relative to each other. It does not decide their lightness, intensity, proportions or readability for you.

Know which colour wheel you are reading

A colour wheel arranges hues in a circle so their relationships are easier to see. Here the diagrams use the digital HSL hue circle: red is at 0°, green at 120° and blue at 240°. The angles are positions, much like directions around a compass. They are not measurements of how attractive a colour is.

Traditional artists’ wheels and digital wheels do not always put the same named colours directly opposite one another. State the model when describing an exact hue relationship. The examples in this guide keep saturation and HSL lightness constant to make the hue positions easier to compare; that does not make the samples equally bright to the eye.

Analogous colours are neighbours

An analogous group draws from nearby positions on the wheel. In this example, orange moves through yellow toward yellow-green. The changes stay within one part of the circle rather than jumping across it. That relationship can provide continuity while still allowing individual colours to differ.

“Nearby” is the main idea, not one compulsory spacing for every analogous palette. If neighbouring colours blend together in a layout, distinguish their lightness, edges or placement. Being related by hue does not ensure that a label will stand out against its background. For a calm group of colours, a difference in value can be more useful than adding another almost identical hue.

Complementary colours sit opposite one another

A complementary pair uses opposite hue positions on the chosen wheel. On the HSL circle shown here, adding 180° to the orange starting hue reaches blue. The diagram highlights that separation; it does not claim that every orange and every blue form an exact pair.

Opposition can make a pairing feel emphatic, but the hue difference alone does not establish useful text contrast. Two strongly coloured areas can still be too similar in lightness for small lettering. Try the pair at the actual size and placement you need, then adjust the specific colours rather than assuming the name “complementary” guarantees success.

Triadic colours are evenly spaced

A triad selects three positions equally spaced around the wheel. In this digital example, each step is 120°: orange, a green and a violet. All three hue families are separated. Compared with the neighbouring group, this gives you a wider variety of colour directions to work with.

Equal spacing concerns the hues, not the amount of space each must occupy in a design. You can keep the full triad and vary the area or strength of each colour. A large green field with smaller orange and violet elements has a different balance from three equal panels, even when the hue positions remain unchanged.

Do not mistake a diagram for a finished palette

Real palettes include lightness differences, muted colours, near-neutrals and sometimes more than one version of a hue. They may suggest a relationship without fitting an exact mathematical pattern. There is no need to force every five-colour set into one harmony label.

Use the terms to make a deliberate comparison. Ask whether you want neighbouring hues, a clear opposition or three separated directions. Then look at the actual colour values and their arrangement. A relationship that is appealing in a row of swatches still needs to work in the finished composition.

When text or controls are involved, check the relevant foreground-background pairs separately. For information carried by colour, add readable labels or other cues. Harmony helps organise hue choices; it does not replace those checks. The useful outcome is a group you can see clearly and recognise as intentional, rather than a diagram that follows a rule perfectly.
